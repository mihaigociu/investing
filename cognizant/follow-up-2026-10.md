# Follow-Up — October 2026: Three Months On

*A follow-up to the July 2026 analysis (committed 2026-07-10, price ~$43). Written 2026-10-09, with CTSH around **~$59**, three weeks before the Q3 print (**Thursday, Oct 29, 2026**). It covers what happened, what the July analysis got right and wrong, and the question the July work left open: **if AI makes people far more productive instead of replacing them, how does Cognizant make money from that?***

> **Sources:** Q2 2026 press release (8-K Ex. 99.1, filed 2026-07-29) and Q2 2026 Form 10-Q (SEC accession 0001058290-26-000031) for every company figure marked *filing*. Call commentary comes from the 2026-07-29 earnings call (via transcript summaries, so check the official IR transcript before quoting it word for word). Price levels come from market-data snapshots taken 2026-10-05 to 10-08 and differ by about ±$1 between sources. Not investment advice.

## Verdict in one line

**The July "wait / small starter" call was right that the crash overshot, but the price never came down to the buy zone. It went the other way: from ~$43 to ~$59 (+37%). At ~$59 the market no longer prices decline. It prices roughly *flat forever*, which is about the base case. The bargain is gone. The business case actually looks a bit better than in July, because the AI shift is showing up in the numbers in the "productivity, not replacement" form you describe. New verdict: HOLD what you have, don't add at ~$59, and set a trim level, mainly because of your concentration risk.**

At the same time management says **20,000+ freshers** will join this year. That is the "broader, shorter pyramid": fewer layers in the middle, more AI-assisted juniors at the bottom.

## 3. Your thesis: AI augments people instead of replacing them. What does that mean for Cognizant?

### First, test the premise
The evidence mostly supports "augment, not replace," with two caveats:
- **At Cognizant:** headcount is still **+3.8% YoY**, and revenue per associate is up 4.6%. That is augmentation, not mass replacement. But headcount fell slightly QoQ, LEAP is cutting mid-level roles, and attrition is rising. The *mix* of people is changing even though the *total* isn't falling.
- **Industry-wide:** payroll studies (Stanford Digital Economy Lab / AI Index 2026, via secondary write-ups) show early-career employment in AI-exposed jobs such as software development down roughly 20% versus trend, while experienced workers held up or grew. Controlled productivity studies range from big gains to none (METR found experienced developers *slower* on familiar code). So the gains are real but uneven, and the cost so far falls on *junior hiring* more than on existing staff.

### Why "augment" is the hard case for an IT-services firm, not the easy one
For Cognizant's business model, "AI makes each engineer 40% more productive" creates a specific problem. If a client needs the same work done, it needs **fewer billable hours**. Mass replacement and mass augmentation lead to the same outcome *on a time-and-materials invoice*. The hour stops being a good unit of price. So the question isn't "will Cognizant still need people?" (it will). It's **who gets paid for the productivity gain: Cognizant or the client?**

The Q2 10-Q answers part of that with real numbers:

| Revenue by contract type (*filing*, $M) | Q2 2025 | Q2 2026 | YoY | H1 YoY | Share Q2'26 (vs Q2'25) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Time & materials** | 2,341 | 2,305 | **−1.5%** | **−1.3%** | 42.1% (44.6%) |
| **Fixed-price** | 2,413 | 2,690 | **+11.5%** | **+12.3%** | 49.1% (46.0%) |
| Transaction / volume | 491 | 486 | −1.0% | +0.3% | 8.9% (9.4%) |
| **Total** | 5,245 | 5,481 | +4.5% | +5.2% | |

In July, T&M was "slowest-growing" (+2.9% over two years). **It is now shrinking in absolute terms.** That is the productivity effect showing up on the hourly-billed book, as the insider read predicted. At the same time, **fixed-price is growing ~12% and is now almost half the company.** (Caveat: some of that fixed-price growth is from the 3Cloud/Astreya acquisitions, and the 10-Q doesn't split it out, so the organic shift is somewhat smaller than it looks.)

### Cognizant's actual strategy: five moves

1. **Stop selling hours. Sell outcomes, and price the AI.** In April, Cognizant introduced **"AI-infused rate cards"** with *human labor* and *digital labor* as separate line items. The CFO calls it "skills plus inference," and it's already used on large deals. Kumar's stated path: billable hours → owning outcomes → *underwriting* outcomes and being paid on them. This is the right response to the problem above: if the hour shrinks, stop charging by the hour.
2. **Share the productivity gain, but keep some of it.** This is the most important line from the Q2 call. Kumar said that, unlike with labor, AI productivity is non-linear, so they can *pass productivity to clients, still beat what they committed, and keep some for themselves*. He also said **margins on $50M+ and $100M+ deals are coming in better than originally signed**. Example: an insurance-brokerage deal that commits to **>50% productivity improvement over five years**. That kind of commitment only pays if Cognizant can deliver it more cheaply than it promised. This is the fixed-price margin argument from July, and management now claims it is working.
3. **Buy higher-value capabilities.** 3Cloud (Microsoft Azure data/AI partner) and Astreya (IT managed services for tech companies). Both are steps away from generic application maintenance.
4. **Reshape the workforce for AI-assisted delivery.** The "broader, shorter pyramid": LEAP cuts in the middle, 20,000+ freshers at the bottom, ~1,000 "context engineers" (built with Workfabric's ContextFabric), plans for 5,000 "Frontier-certified" engineers and 10,000 business operators, and an **"AI fluency meter"** that scores every associate (relevant to *you*: it reportedly feeds into evaluations). 40%+ of software development is now AI-assisted, and there are 8,000+ AI engagements.
5. **Bet that cheaper delivery creates more demand.** This is the hidden assumption behind "the market opportunity is larger than ever." If AI makes modernization projects ~40% cheaper, clients may start the backlog of projects they had been putting off. Evidence so far: Accenture +5% local currency for the year with a 3–6% FY27 guide, TCS +3.2% cc (with a $2.6B AI revenue run-rate), CTSH ~3% organic. **Demand hasn't collapsed, but it hasn't boomed either.** Management itself says two-thirds of the Global 2000 haven't seen measurable AI gains yet, and one in four have *paused* AI deployments.

### My assessment of the positioning
- **The direction is right, and it's the only strategy that works.** Moving to fixed-price and outcome pricing, with AI as a priced input, is how a labor-model firm keeps the productivity gain instead of losing it. The numbers show the shift is real (fixed-price up ~12%, adj. margin up 40 bps while T&M shrinks), not just slides.
- **But none of this is a moat. Every competitor runs the same playbook.** Accenture, TCS, Infosys, and HCL all have the same rate-card and outcome-pricing approach. When everyone can offer "50% productivity over five years," clients make vendors **compete on how much of the gain they give back**. That pushes the "sticky but price-squeezed" problem *into the contract terms*: it shows up in the next bid, not in this quarter's margin. Margins being better than signed holds for deals signed *before* the bidding caught up. The test is renewals in 2027–28.
- **Fixed-price moves risk onto Cognizant.** Underwriting outcomes means eating overruns. When productivity assumptions change every quarter, a bad bid is easy to make. Nothing has gone wrong yet, but this risk didn't exist under T&M.
- **The growth is narrow.** FS +11.7% is carrying the company while the other three segments sit around +1%. One strong segment plus acquisitions is weaker evidence than broad-based organic growth.

**Net:** Cognizant is positioning *competently* for the augmentation world, roughly at the level of its peers rather than ahead of them. July scored the moat **narrow and eroding**. I'd now say **narrow, but erosion is slowing**: the shift to fixed-price shows the company can move its revenue to contracts where productivity helps it, and that was the main open question in July. It's still not a widening moat, because the advantage is a playbook anyone can copy.

## 4. Re-valuation at ~$59

**Updated anchors:** shares **450.4M**, market cap **~$26.6B**, net debt **~$0.5B** → **EV ~$27.1B**. FY26 FCF ~$2.6–2.8B (H1 flat YoY; LEAP severance is a cash drag this year). Owner earnings ~**$2.5B** (FCF − ~$0.2B SBC) → **~$5.55/share** (up from $5.24, mostly from the lower share count).

| Lens | July (~$43) | **Now (~$59)** |
|---|---:|---:|
| P/E (FY26 adj. EPS guide midpoint $5.76) | ~7.6x | **~10.2x** |
| EV / FCF | ~7.3x | **~10.0–10.8x** |
| Owner-earnings yield | ~12% | **~9.4%** |
| Dividend yield ($0.33 × 4) | ~3.0% | **~2.2%** |
| **Reverse-DCF implied perpetual growth** (10% discount rate) | **−3.7%/yr** | **~0% (−1% to +1%)** |

**The key shift:** in July the market priced **decline**. Now it prices **stagnation**, which is close to the July base case. The asymmetry that made $43 interesting ("you win even if it shrinks a bit") has mostly gone. At $59 you need the business to *hold steady* just to earn the ~10% discount rate. You no longer get paid for being less pessimistic than the market.

**Intrinsic value, re-run with the July scenario assumptions** (10% discount rate, new share count, net debt):

| Scenario | July | Now | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Bear (−6% → −4% → −3% terminal) | $30–35 | **~$32–35** | ~unchanged |
| Base (0% → +1% → +1%) | $50–55 | **~$53–58** | slightly up (buybacks, plus a slightly lower probability of the bear case) |
| Bull (+4% → +4% → +2.5%) | $65–75 | **~$70–80** | slightly up |

The July haircuts are kept. A plain mechanical DCF gives ~$59 base and ~$83 bull, so the table already leans conservative. The fewer-shares gain is mostly offset by the swing from net cash to net debt. Value per share went up because of buybacks and slightly better evidence, not because the business grew a lot.

**So at ~$59: about at base-case fair value, above the old buy price, well below the bull case.** Fairly priced, not cheap and not expensive.

## 5. Updated decision

### Verdict: **HOLD. Don't add at ~$59. Set a trim level.**

| If you… | Then |
|---|---|
| **Bought the July starter (~$43)** | Hold it. You're up ~37% plus a dividend. The thesis is working, and the AI evidence is better than in July. Selling now would just be a trade. |
| **Didn't buy** | Don't chase it. At fair value you're paying for the base case and getting no margin of safety. The new, *looser* add zone is **≤ ~$45–48** (a ~20% discount to the ~$56 base). That's less than July's 35–40%, because the AI evidence has improved and §2 showed the July bar was too strict. |
| **Hold a lot of CTSH (RSUs/ESPP)** | **This is the most relevant row for you.** The rally is a good moment to **reduce concentration**. LEAP shows your employer is restructuring around AI, so your *job* and your *stock* now depend on the same bet. Trimming vested RSUs above ~$65–70 (the low end of the bull range) is defensible even if you like the company. Check the trading window/blackout calendar first: Q3 is Oct 29, and you're probably in a closed window now. |

### What to watch on Oct 29 (Q3) and the next two quarters
1. **T&M revenue decline rate vs. fixed-price growth.** Healthy: T&M −1% to −3% while fixed-price grows ≥8% *organically*. Bad: T&M falling faster than −5%, or fixed-price growth fading once the acquisitions are lapped.
2. **Adj. margin holding at ≥16% while LEAP runs.** This shows whether Cognizant is keeping some of the productivity gain or handing it all back.
3. **Organic growth outside Financial Services.** Health Sciences and P&R need to move off ~1%.
4. **Single-quarter bookings.** Q2 was −6%. A second negative quarter would weaken the "longer contracts" explanation.
5. **Net debt and the October 2027 revolver/term-loan maturity.** Is the revolver draw being paid down from FCF, or rolled into more buybacks?
6. **Headcount and attrition.** Revenue up while headcount is flat or down is the *good* version of augmentation. Rising attrition (13.0% now) is the risk to the talent base.
7. **A disclosed AI revenue number.** TCS reports one ($2.6B run-rate). Cognizant reports engagement counts and pipeline. Not disclosing it is a minor candor point.
